# Reducing friction

Even a visitor who wants to act can be stopped by small frictions: a form that asks too much, an unclear next step, a slow page, a moment of doubt. Friction is everything between the visitor's intent and the completed action, and much of it is invisible to the page's owner because they already know the answers the visitor is missing. Removing friction is often the cheapest way to lift conversion, because it recovers visitors who were already willing.

## Ask for as little as possible

Every field in a form, every piece of information you require, is a small cost that some visitors will not pay. A form asking for one piece of information converts better than one asking for five, because each additional field adds effort and a reason to abandon. The discipline is to ask only for what you genuinely need at this step, and to defer the rest to later, after the visitor has committed. If you only need an email to start, ask only for an email. Collecting more than you need now, for convenience later, costs conversions now. Match the ask to the commitment level the action actually requires.

## Make the next step unmistakable

Friction hides in ambiguity. A visitor who is not certain what happens when they click, what they are committing to, or what comes next, hesitates. The call to action should say plainly what it does, so the visitor knows exactly what clicking means. Vague labels create a moment of doubt; specific ones remove it. The same applies to what happens after: if the action begins a process, telling the visitor what to expect reduces the uncertainty that makes people hesitate at the threshold. Clarity about the step itself is a form of friction removal, because confusion is friction.

## Speed is friction

A page that loads slowly loses visitors before they see it, and a sluggish, janky page frustrates the ones who stay. Page speed is a direct conversion factor: every second of delay sheds some visitors who will not wait. This is partly a technical matter, covered by the core-web-vitals reference, but it belongs in any discussion of friction because it is among the most common and most fixable causes of lost conversions. A fast, responsive page removes a friction the visitor never consciously notices but reacts to by leaving.

## Anticipate and answer doubts

As a visitor moves toward acting, doubts surface: is this safe, what if it is not right, what is the catch, can I trust this. Friction accumulates when these doubts go unanswered and the visitor leaves to think about it, which usually means leaving for good. Anticipating the common doubts and answering them near the point of action, what happens with their information, whether they can change their mind, what the real cost is, removes the reason to pause. The doubts are predictable; addressing them on the page, rather than leaving the visitor to worry, keeps momentum toward the action.

## Test the path, not just the page

The full path to conversion often extends beyond the landing page itself, into a form, a checkout, a confirmation, and friction anywhere along it costs you. A clean landing page that hands off to a clumsy multi-step process leaks the visitors it convinced. Walking the entire path as a visitor would, from arrival to completed action, surfaces the frictions that a focus on the page alone misses: the confusing form field, the unexpected required step, the unclear confirmation. Reducing friction means smoothing the whole journey to the goal, not just polishing the page where it starts.
