# The above-the-fold decision

The part of the page a visitor sees without scrolling, often called above the fold, carries an outsized share of the work, because it is where the visitor decides in a few seconds whether to stay and read or leave. If that first view does not quickly answer what this is, who it is for, and why it matters, most visitors are gone before they reach anything below. Getting the top of the page right is the highest-impact thing you can do.

## Answer the visitor's first questions immediately

In the first seconds, a visitor is asking, mostly without realizing it: what is this, is it for me, and what do I get. The top of the page has to answer all three fast. A clear headline states what the offer is and the benefit it delivers, in the visitor's terms rather than yours. A short supporting line adds the essential detail. Together they should let a stranger understand the offer and recognize whether it fits them, in one quick read. Cleverness that delays understanding is the enemy here; clarity that delivers it instantly is the goal.

## Lead with the benefit, not the mechanism

Visitors care about what the offer does for them before they care about how it works. The headline should lead with the outcome or benefit, the thing the visitor wants, rather than with features or internal detail. Features and mechanics matter and have their place further down, but the first thing a visitor sees should connect to their goal, because that is what makes them decide the page is worth their attention. A top that opens with technical detail or company-centric language loses visitors who have not yet been given a reason to care.

## Make the action visible at the top

The call to action, the thing you want the visitor to do, should be present and clear in the first view, not hidden below a long scroll. A visitor who is convinced quickly should be able to act immediately without hunting. This does not mean the action is the only thing above the fold, but it should be visible, so that the path from understanding to acting is short for those who are ready. Burying the action makes ready visitors work to find it, and some will not bother. The top view should both explain and offer the next step.

## Respect that most people will not scroll far

A large share of visitors never scroll much past the first view, so anything essential that lives only far down the page reaches few people. This argues for putting the core of your case, the offer, the key benefit, the primary proof, and the action, near the top, with the deeper detail below for those who want it. The page can be long, and length is fine for visitors who want to read more, but the page must work for the visitor who reads only the top. Design for the short attention first, the long attention second.

## Test the top hardest

Because the above-the-fold view does so much of the work, it is where changes move conversion most, and therefore where testing pays off most. The headline especially is worth iterating on, since a clearer or more relevant headline can shift the whole page's performance. When improving a landing page, the top view is the first place to look: is it instantly clear, does it lead with benefit, is the action visible, does it match what brought the visitor. Fixing a vague or buried top often does more than any amount of polishing further down.
