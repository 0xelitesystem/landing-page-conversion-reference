# One page, one goal

The most reliable way to lower a landing page's conversion is to give it more than one goal. Every additional action you offer competes with the primary one, splits the visitor's attention, and dilutes the page's focus. A page that asks a visitor to choose between several things converts worse on each than a page that asks for one thing clearly. Focus is not a stylistic preference; it is a conversion lever.

## Competing actions cancel out

When a page offers several calls to action, sign up, but also download this, and also read that, and also follow us, the visitor faces a choice rather than a clear next step, and choices create hesitation. A confused or hesitating visitor often does nothing at all. By removing the competing options and presenting one obvious action, you remove the hesitation and make the decision simple: do this, or leave. This is why dedicated landing pages strip away the navigation and the side offers that a general site carries. Each thing you remove that is not the goal strengthens the goal.

## Remove the navigation and the exits

A general website's navigation menu is a set of invitations to go elsewhere, which is the opposite of what a landing page wants. A focused landing page often removes the standard navigation entirely, because every menu link is a way for the visitor to wander off before acting. The same applies to other exits: links to your other products, your blog, your social accounts. On a landing page, these are leaks, not features. Sealing the leaks keeps the visitor's attention on the one decision the page exists to prompt. The page can feel sparse compared to a homepage, and that sparseness is doing work.

## One primary action, repeated not multiplied

One goal does not mean the action appears only once. On a longer page, repeating the same call to action at sensible points, so a visitor convinced at any point can act without scrolling back, helps. The distinction is between repeating one action and offering several different ones. Repeating the same signup button down the page is good; offering a signup, a download, and a demo booking is not. Keep the action consistent and available, while keeping it singular. The visitor should always know exactly what the one next step is, no matter where they are on the page.

## A secondary path can exist, quietly

There is a narrow exception: a small secondary option for visitors who are not ready for the main action, such as a low-commitment alternative for those not prepared to commit fully. This can capture people who would otherwise leave with nothing. But it must be clearly secondary, visually quieter and subordinate to the primary action, so it does not compete. The moment a secondary option looks as prominent as the primary one, you are back to competing actions. Used sparingly and kept small, a single quiet fallback is fine; anything more reintroduces the dilution the focus was meant to remove.

## Focus clarifies everything else

When a page has one goal, every other decision gets easier, because you can ask of any element whether it helps the visitor take that one action. Copy, proof, images, layout, all get judged against the single purpose, and anything that does not serve it can go. This is why one-goal pages tend to be cleaner and clearer: the constraint forces out the clutter. A page that tries to do several things has no such test and accumulates elements that each seemed reasonable but collectively confuse. One goal is both a conversion lever and a design discipline that keeps the page coherent.
