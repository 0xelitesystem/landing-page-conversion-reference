# What a landing page is for

A landing page is built around a single action. Someone arrives, usually from an ad, a link, a post, or an email, and the page has one job: get them to take that specific next step. That focus is what makes a landing page different from a general website, and it is also what most underperforming pages lose sight of. The page is not there to explain everything about you; it is there to convert this visitor on this one thing.

## One page, one action

The defining feature of a landing page is that it is dedicated to one goal. Everything on it should serve that goal: the headline, the explanation, the proof, the call to action. A page trying to get newsletter signups is doing a different job from one trying to sell a product, and mixing the two weakens both. The clarity of purpose is the page's strength. When you can state in one sentence what you want the visitor to do, and every element on the page supports that sentence, the page is doing its job. When you cannot, the page is unfocused, and unfocused pages convert poorly.

## How it differs from a homepage

A homepage serves many visitors with many intents and offers many paths: learn about the company, browse products, find support, read the blog. That breadth suits a homepage and ruins a landing page. A landing page receives visitors with a specific context, they clicked a specific ad or link about a specific thing, and it should meet that context with a focused offer, not a menu of everything. Sending ad traffic to a general homepage, where the visitor has to hunt for the thing the ad promised, wastes the click. The landing page continues the specific promise the visitor responded to.

## Match the page to the source

Because visitors arrive from a specific source with a specific expectation, the page should match what brought them. If an ad promised a particular benefit, the page should lead with that benefit, not a generic pitch. This continuity, often called message match, matters because a mismatch creates doubt: the visitor clicked expecting one thing and landed on something that does not clearly connect, so they leave. The tighter the alignment between the promise that earned the click and what the page delivers, the higher the conversion, because the visitor immediately sees they are in the right place.

## The visitor decides fast

People judge a page in seconds and leave if it does not quickly show them they are in the right place and that the offer is worth their attention. This speed of judgment shapes everything: the most important information has to be immediate, the action has to be obvious, and anything that delays understanding costs visitors. A landing page does not have the luxury of unfolding slowly; it has to make its case fast, because the visitor's patience is short and the back button is one tap away. The next pages are largely about respecting that speed.

## Conversion is the measure

A landing page is judged by one number: the share of visitors who take the action. This is liberating, because it makes the page's job measurable and improvable. You can test changes against that number and know whether they helped. It is also disciplining, because it cuts through opinions about design and copy to the only question that matters, did more people act. Keeping the conversion goal explicit, and measuring against it, is what turns landing page work from decoration into something you can actually improve.
